This is the how-to that goes with [[Too many ideas, three at a time]]. I keep all of it in Obsidian, but nothing here depends on the app. It works on paper too.

## The layers

Everything hangs off five layers, from the long term down to today:

1. **Vision**: one page about the life I'm working towards. I rarely touch it.
2. **Goals**: the big outcomes, each with a horizon. Short is under a year, mid is one to three years, long is three to five, and a few are ten-year goals. Every goal points to the one above it, so I can always see why something matters.
3. **The 12-week cycle**: at most **three** goals I'm pushing forward right now.
4. **Projects**: the concrete work that moves a cycle goal. Each project lists its next steps with dates.
5. **The weekly plan**: one page, with the newest week on top.

The trick is that each layer only looks one level up. On a Tuesday I don't think about my ten-year vision. I look at this week's page, which points to projects, which point to the cycle.

## The cycle

A cycle is twelve weeks of work plus a thirteenth week for review and rest. Mine run four times a year. The current one is 28 September to 20 December, and the review week comes straight after it.

At the start of a cycle I pick up to three goals and write down, for each one:

- **What done looks like** by the end of week 12, in one or two sentences.
- **The projects** that will get it there.
- **A simple score**, which I fill in during the weekly review. It's the share of planned tasks I actually did that week.

Around 80% is a good week. If you're hitting 100% every week, you're planning too little. Below 60% for a few weeks means the plan is wrong, not me, and I cut something.

## Projects and their status

Every project has one status:

- **focus**: being worked on this cycle (no more than three)
- **active**: moving, but not the priority
- **next**: first in line for the next cycle
- **parked**: a good idea, not now
- **done**

"Parked" is the most important one. New ideas turn up every week. Instead of starting them, I create the project, write two lines about it, mark it parked, and get back to work. Nothing is lost, and nothing steals the cycle.

## The weekly page

I use one page for the whole planning period, with the newest week at the top. I don't keep a separate page per day, because it's lighter and I can see the whole cycle by scrolling.

Each week starts with:

- **Three focus tasks** for the week, pulled from focus projects.
- **Carried**: anything unfinished from last week. I either carry it again, send it back to its project, or drop it. I mark those `[>]`, `[<]` and `[-]` so I can see my own patterns.

Each day is a small block with the same shape, which I call 3-3-3:

- **One deep-work block** (about three hours) on the most important thing.
- **Three short tasks** that move something forward.
- **Three maintenance tasks**: admin, email, errands.

On clinical days the deep-work block is often the job itself, and that's fine. The structure is there to protect the work, not to make me feel guilty.

## The weekly review

On the last day of the week, in about twenty minutes:

1. Tick what's done and fill in the score.
2. Deal with every unfinished task: carry it, send it back or drop it.
3. Check each focus project. Is it still the right thing?
4. Write three focus tasks for next week.

That's the whole ritual. I tried heavier versions with long journals and daily reviews, and I always stopped doing them within a month. Light rituals are the ones that survive.

## The log

Anything worth remembering (a decision, a lesson, a number) goes into a single running log for the year, with a date. When I close a project, the relevant log entries get linked from it. That's how the vault stays useful instead of turning into a junk drawer.

## If you want to try it

Start small:

1. Write down every goal you have, then pick **three** for the next twelve weeks.
2. Mark everything else as parked. Don't delete it.
3. Make one page for your weeks and plan just this one: three focus tasks.
4. Review on Friday.

Do that for a single cycle before adding anything else. Most of the value comes from the limit, not from the tools.
