**Oscar Wilde** · 1895 · Play
<span class="book-shelf">Want to read</span>

The Importance of Being Earnest is a comic play, first staged in London in 1895, which Oscar Wilde subtitled 'A Trivial Comedy for Serious People'. Jack Worthing lives a respectable life in the country but escapes to London by pretending to be his invented wayward brother, Ernest. His friend Algernon Moncrieff has a similar trick: an imaginary sick friend called Bunbury who gets him out of dull social duties.

Complications multiply when both men fall for young women who are charmed by the name Ernest, and when Lady Bracknell, the formidable mother of Jack's beloved Gwendolen, questions him about his unusual origins. Through quick, epigrammatic dialogue, Wilde mocks Victorian ideas of marriage, class and respectability, suggesting that society values style and appearances more than sincerity.

## Key insights

- Double lives are exhausting. Both men's invented identities create more trouble than they solve, a comic warning about the cost of keeping up pretences.
- Societies that prize appearances reward performance over character. Wilde presents respectability as a set of manners rather than moral substance.
- Humour can carry serious criticism. By keeping the audience laughing, Wilde exposes snobbery and hypocrisy without ever preaching.
- Inverting expectations sharpens wit. Wilde's characters treat trivial things seriously and serious things lightly, which shows how arbitrary many social rules are.
- Labels can matter absurdly. The women's fixation on the name Ernest satirises romantic ideals built on surface qualities rather than real knowledge of a person.

*Read it if you want a short, very funny play whose sharp dialogue still skewers snobbery and social pretence.*
