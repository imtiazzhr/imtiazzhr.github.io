**Khaled Hosseini** · 2003 · Novel
<span class="book-shelf">Read</span>

The Kite Runner is narrated by Amir, who grows up in a wealthy household in Kabul in the 1970s. His closest companion is Hassan, the fiercely loyal son of his father's servant and a member of the Hazara minority. Amir longs for his father's approval, and after a kite-fighting tournament in the winter of 1975 he fails Hassan at a crucial moment, a betrayal that haunts him for decades.

After the Soviet invasion, Amir and his father flee to California and build a new life. Years later, a phone call from an old family friend offers Amir a chance to make amends, drawing him back to an Afghanistan now ruled by the Taliban. The novel explores guilt, redemption, friendship, fathers and sons, and ethnic and class divisions.

## Key insights

- Guilt that is avoided does not fade. Amir's attempts to escape what he did only deepen its hold on him until he faces it.
- Redemption usually requires action and cost, not just remorse. Making amends means taking real risks to repair what can still be repaired.
- A child's hunger for a parent's approval can drive both ambition and moral failure, as Amir's relationship with his father shows.
- Loyalty is not always returned in equal measure. Hassan's steadfast devotion contrasts with Amir's weakness and invites readers to consider what they owe those who stand by them.
- Prejudice shapes even close relationships. The Hazaras' lower status in Afghan society colours every interaction between the two boys.

*Read it if you want a moving story of guilt, friendship and redemption across decades of Afghan history, and can handle distressing scenes.*
