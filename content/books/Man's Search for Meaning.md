**Viktor E. Frankl** · 1946 · Psychology
<span class="book-shelf">Read</span>

Viktor Frankl, a Viennese psychiatrist, survived several Nazi concentration camps, including Auschwitz, during the Second World War. The first part of this short book describes camp life from a psychologist's point of view: the shock of arrival, the apathy that followed, and the difficult return to ordinary life after liberation. Frankl observed that prisoners who held on to a reason to live, such as a loved one or unfinished work, were more likely to endure.

The second part introduces logotherapy, his school of psychotherapy, which treats the search for meaning as a basic human drive. He argues that meaning can be found through work or creation, through love and experience, and through the attitude we take towards suffering we cannot avoid.

## Key insights

- Meaning can come from three sources: meaningful work, love and experience, and the attitude you choose towards suffering you cannot change.
- Even when almost everything is taken away, a person keeps the freedom to choose their attitude. Frankl calls it the last human freedom.
- Having a clear why, such as someone waiting or work to finish, makes hardship more bearable. Name yours before a crisis arrives.
- Happiness and success cannot be pursued directly; they follow as by-products of commitment to a cause or a person beyond yourself.
- Feelings of emptiness and boredom, which Frankl calls the existential vacuum, often signal a lack of meaning rather than a lack of pleasure.

*Read it if you want a short, humane account of surviving extreme suffering and a practical framework for finding purpose.*
