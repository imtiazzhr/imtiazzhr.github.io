**George Orwell** · 1945 · Classic novel
<span class="book-shelf">Read</span>

On Manor Farm, the animals are stirred by an old boar's vision of a life without human masters. They drive out their neglectful, drunken owner, Mr Jones, and set up their own society under seven commandments that promise equality for all animals. Because the pigs are the cleverest, they take charge of organising the work and deciding policy.

Orwell's short fable follows what happens as the pigs, led by Napoleon and aided by the smooth-talking Squealer, gradually gather power and bend the rules. Written as an allegory of the Russian Revolution and the rise of Stalin, it struggled to find a publisher while Britain was allied with the Soviet Union. It remains a sharp study of propaganda, power and betrayed ideals.

## Key insights

- Watch who controls the rules; the commandments are altered a few words at a time, and few animals can read well enough to notice.
- Propaganda works best on the tired and busy; Squealer's figures and speeches target animals too exhausted to check his claims.
- Loyalty without scrutiny is easily exploited; Boxer meets every problem by resolving to work harder, and the pigs reap the benefit.
- Slogans can drown out debate; the sheep's chanting silences discussion at exactly the moments when careful thought is most needed.
- The danger lies in unchecked power itself, not just in who holds it; a revolution can end up reproducing the tyranny it replaced.

*Read it if you want a short, clear fable about how propaganda works and how ideals can be quietly betrayed.*
