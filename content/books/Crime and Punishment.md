**Fyodor Dostoevsky** · 1866 · Classic novel
<span class="book-shelf">Want to read</span>

Rodion Raskolnikov, a proud and penniless former student in St Petersburg, has persuaded himself that exceptional people may break moral rules in the service of a greater good. To test the idea, he murders an elderly pawnbroker he considers harmful and worthless, and then, in panic, kills her sister as well. The crime happens early; the novel is about what follows.

Raskolnikov sinks into fever, paranoia and inner conflict. He is circled by Porfiry Petrovich, a shrewd investigator, and drawn to Sonya Marmeladova, a devout young woman forced into prostitution to support her family. Dostoevsky, who first published the novel in instalments in 1866, uses the story to examine guilt, poverty, pride and the limits of reason as a guide to living.

## Key insights

- Theories that justify harm for a higher cause tend to collapse under the weight of conscience, however neat they look on paper.
- Punishment begins inside; Raskolnikov's guilt, dread and isolation torment him long before the law can reach him.
- Pride isolates; Raskolnikov pushes away the people who care about him, which makes his inner crisis far worse.
- Patience can be more powerful than force; Porfiry lets the suspect's own restless mind do much of the investigator's work.
- Compassion can reach people whom argument cannot; Sonya's humble faith affects Raskolnikov more deeply than any debate.

*Read it if you want a gripping psychological novel about guilt, conscience and whether an idea can ever justify doing harm.*
