**Benjamin Franklin** · 1791 · Memoir
<span class="book-shelf">Currently reading</span>

Franklin began this memoir in 1771 as a letter to his son and returned to it in several stages until shortly before his death. It follows him from a modest Boston childhood and an apprenticeship in his older brother's printing shop, to his arrival in Philadelphia as a teenager with almost nothing, and on to success as a printer, publisher and civic organiser.

Much of its appeal lies in his account of deliberate self-improvement. He describes teaching himself to write, forming a club of friends for mutual improvement, and helping to found a subscription library, a fire company and other public institutions. He also sets out his plan for practising thirteen virtues. The account ends in the late 1750s, long before his role in American independence.

## Key insights

- Franklin worked on thirteen virtues by focusing on one each week and marking lapses in a small chart. Tackling a single habit at a time made progress visible.
- He planned each day on paper, opening with a question about what good he would do and closing with a review of what good he had done.
- He learned to write by summarising essays he admired, rewriting them later from his notes and comparing the results. Imitation with honest feedback is a strong teacher.
- Franklin stopped stating opinions as absolute certainties and framed them as his current view. He found people argued less and cooperated more readily.
- When proposing public projects, he presented them as the idea of a group of friends rather than his own, which reduced envy and won wider support.

*Read it if you enjoy practical self-improvement and want to see how one curious, disciplined person shaped his character and career.*
