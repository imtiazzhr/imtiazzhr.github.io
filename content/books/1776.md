**David McCullough** · 2005 · History
<span class="book-shelf">Read</span>

David McCullough tells the story of one year of the American Revolutionary War, focusing on soldiers and generals rather than the politicians in Philadelphia. He follows George Washington and his inexperienced Continental Army from the siege of Boston, where cannon hauled from Fort Ticonderoga forced the British to leave, through a run of heavy defeats around New York. He also portrays the British side, including King George III and General William Howe, as complex people rather than villains.

By the end of the year the army was shrinking and the cause looked close to collapse. McCullough shows how Washington's persistence, able young officers such as Nathanael Greene and Henry Knox, and some good fortune led to surprise victories at Trenton and Princeton that kept the Revolution alive.

## Key insights

- Leaders can make serious mistakes and still succeed. Washington misjudged the defence of New York, yet he learned from defeat and kept the army together.
- Talent can come from unlikely places. Henry Knox was a Boston bookseller and Nathanael Greene a Rhode Island forge owner, both largely self-taught soldiers.
- Chance shapes history. Fog covering the night retreat from Brooklyn saved the army at a moment when strategy had failed.
- Morale can matter as much as numbers; a small, well-timed victory restored belief and helped persuade soldiers to stay on.
- History looks inevitable only in hindsight. In 1776 many people expected the Revolution to collapse, which makes its survival feel contingent and human.

*Read it if you want a readable narrative of leadership and endurance during a critical year of the American Revolution.*
