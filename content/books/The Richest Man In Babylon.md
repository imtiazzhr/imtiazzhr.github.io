**George S. Clason** · 1926 · Personal finance
<span class="book-shelf">Read</span>

George S. Clason began writing parables set in ancient Babylon in the 1920s, and banks and insurance companies distributed them as pamphlets to teach basic money habits. Collected as a book, they remain popular for their simple, memorable lessons.

The central figure is Arkad, a humble scribe who became the wealthiest man in the city. He explains to friends that wealth begins by keeping a part of everything you earn, at least a tenth, before spending the rest. Other stories cover controlling expenses, putting savings to work, avoiding foolish investments, owning a home, providing for old age and increasing your ability to earn. Later tales show a debtor turning his life around with a disciplined repayment plan, and a slave rising to prosperity through hard work.

## Key insights

- Pay yourself first. Set aside at least a tenth of every income before paying bills, and treat that saving as money you do not touch.
- Separate necessary expenses from desires. Spending tends to rise to match income unless you budget deliberately and question each purchase.
- Put savings to work so they earn income, then reinvest that income. Money kept working compounds, while money left idle or spent does not.
- Protect your capital before chasing returns. Take investment advice from people experienced in that field, not from well-meaning friends outside it.
- To clear debt, one character lives on seven tenths of his income, saves one tenth and divides the remaining two tenths among his creditors until they are repaid.

*Read it if you want simple, lasting principles of saving and money management told through short, memorable stories.*
