**Tara Westover** · 2018 · Memoir
<span class="book-shelf">Currently reading</span>

Tara Westover grew up on a mountain in rural Idaho, the youngest of seven children in a survivalist family that distrusted government, doctors and public schools. She never went to school, spent her teenage years working in her father's scrapyard, and saw serious injuries treated at home with her mother's herbal remedies. One of her brothers became violent towards her.

Encouraged by an older brother who had left for university, she taught herself enough to pass a university entrance test and started at Brigham Young University at seventeen, having never sat in a classroom. Her studies later took her to Cambridge, where she earned a doctorate in history. The memoir weighs what education gave her against what it cost, including a painful break with parts of her family.

## Key insights

- Education is more than information; for Westover it meant gaining the ability to question the story she had been told about her own life.
- Gaps in knowledge can be invisible; not knowing a basic historical term at university showed her how upbringing limits understanding.
- Memory is unreliable; she notes where siblings recall events differently, a model of honesty about the limits of recollection.
- You can love your family and still choose distance; loyalty should not require denying your own experience of harm.
- Belief in your potential can be borrowed at first; mentors saw her abilities before she did, and their confidence helped her grow into them.

*Read it if you are interested in how education can remake a life, and in the personal costs that such change can bring.*
